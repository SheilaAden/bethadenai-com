The website was almost ready to take orders. We still needed to confirm what was in the warehouse, which products the site could offer, and how the warehouse would pack aerosols for shipment.

Those sound like little details until somebody pays you for something you can’t ship.

## Follow an order from checkout to shipment

On a [product relaunch I helped coordinate](/work/product-relaunch), the website, inventory, and fulfillment teams each had work to finish. The tricky part was that one team's decision changed what another team needed to do. The web team needed confirmed inventory before setting availability. Fulfillment needed an agreed packing method. Whoever gave the go-ahead needed to know those steps were complete and see what happened when a test order went through.

My job was to make those handoffs visible and get the people who could answer the questions together. The team updated the inventory count and confirmed how aerosols would be packed. That gave us a clearer picture of what still needed to happen before opening orders.

## Put the handoff on the launch checklist

Pick one real item and walk it all the way through. Who confirms the quantity? Who puts that quantity on the site? When an order goes through, who sees it, who packs it, and how does the customer find out it shipped? Run a test order and have the people doing the work tell you where the answers get fuzzy.

For each gap, write down who owns the next action, what they need, and who’s waiting on the result. An AI tool can help turn a meeting recap into that list. The people doing the work still have to check that the list matches reality.

I use the same approach when the stakes are a field transition. In a [paving bid and work-order transition](/work/paving-bid-and-transition), one question was where the next work order would go while crews still had open jobs to finish. Follow the next order and you’ll see exactly where the teams have to connect.
